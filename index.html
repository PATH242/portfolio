Design thinking workshop · HPI d-school · September 2026

Making an AI receptionist legible to patients

Praxipal builds Luna, an AI assistant that answers phone calls for medical practices. In some practices over 80% of callers accept her. In others it is about 30%. Nobody could say why.

My role
Part of the problem investigation, helped design the interview questions and ran several interviews. Then led the redesign of Luna’s call introduction, from script to test.
Team
Five students and a design thinking coach, over five days.
Partner
Praxipal, Germany.
Outcome
Praxipal added the new introduction to their own version for testing and saw promising early signs.

The problem

At the start of every call, Luna asks the caller to agree to speak with an AI. Praxipal counts acceptance as the share of callers who say yes. Their analysis of 144 practices found almost no signal in patient age, insurance mix, town size or geography, and the settings a practice can change are nearly identical everywhere.

80% to 30%acceptance range across practices
144practices analysed, no demographic explanation
~70%of the variance outside anything measured

Our brief: how might we increase patient acceptance when interacting with an AI voice assistant in medical practices?

How we looked into it

We called Luna ourselves over two days and compared her with other AI voice products. Then we ran nine interviews: seven patients aged 20 to 90, an orthodontist’s assistant and a Praxipal representative. Several patients were relatives and friends, recruited within a day, so we treat what we found as directional.

  • Three of seven patients, in different age groups, raised data privacy without being asked. The practice had never heard it from callers.
  • Patients accepted AI for simple tasks. What they objected to was being stuck with it when the task was not simple, and many were unsure what Luna could and could not do.
  • The practice assistant said 10 to 15% of callers refuse to give a name and date of birth, which ends the booking.

Patients do not need Luna to be more human. They need the rules of the conversation to be legible before and during the call.

Every need we dot-voted pointed the same way: a guaranteed human fallback, clarity about scope, knowing why Luna is used. These are information problems. The cheapest places to solve them are before the call and in its first seconds.

What I designed: a new opening for every call

Praxipal already lets practices change the introduction, so this needed no engineering on the voice model. Two things are fixed by law. Luna must say she is an AI, and she must ask for the caller’s agreement. I built the rest around those, and it had to fit in about ten seconds, since Luna is billed by the minute and long-windedness was the most common complaint we heard.

Inform

“This is Luna, the AI booking assistant.”

Meets the legal duty to say she is an AI, and says what she is for, in one breath. The name stays, but never on its own.

Reassure

“I’m happy to help with booking and questions on data protection.”

Patients brought up privacy unprompted, so the intro raises it first. An open invitation shows the question is welcome.

Hand off

“For complex questions, I’ll redirect you to the practice team.”

Names the limit and the way out together, which is what patients said they needed.

Ask consent

“May I help you?”

Meets the legal duty to ask for the caller’s agreement before going on.

Wording is the whole idea, so I wrote it first as a script, then recorded it with a voice model in an ElevenLabs instance Praxipal gave us, so testers heard it the way a patient would.

What testing showed

We tested on day four with coaches from other teams and showed it to our contact at Praxipal. Coaches liked that it set expectations. They also raised two problems. A caller can skip past an introduction they have heard before, and the name “Luna” makes her sound personal at the moment we want to say she is not a person. They suggested stating the request first.

Praxipal’s contact found the introduction interesting and added it to their own version to test. We saw promising early signs.

Teammates also built a second prototype, a life-size cardboard Luna for waiting rooms. Testers found it awkward to phone from a waiting room, which taught us that physical material should build awareness, not ask for a call on the spot. I did not work on that one.

What comes next

Run the new introduction as an A/B test across two comparable practices and compare completed bookings with abandoned calls. First the team needs to agree what a successful call is. Because Luna is billed by the minute, call length cannot stand in for acceptance. We suggested a completed booking or a handoff to a human.

Product prototype · Make Your Case, HPI d-school · 2026, in progress

The Quiet Streak

A phone widget for language learners that counts the words you recalled, not the days you opened the app.

My role
Concept, interaction design and the working prototype below.
Context
A course on driving technical design decisions with design thinking.
Status
Tested with friends and colleagues, testing and refining is work in progress.

The idea

A streak rewards showing up. Learning needs repeated exposure to new words and a way to check what stuck. The widget sits on the lock screen and home screen, so a learner meets words during the day without opening the app, and the number that counts is words recalled.

Decisions built into the prototype

  • New words do not score. A new word appears with an example sentence. Seeing it once is exposure, so the only action is “Noted”.
  • Known words start hidden. The learner taps to see the word used, then answers “I knew it” or “Not yet”. Only honest recall counts, and “Not yet” brings the word back three cards later.
  • Glances are not learning. A card nobody touches rotates away after five glances and goes back into the queue.
  • The learner sets the bar. At today’s target of five recalled words, the widget asks whether to raise it by one or keep it.
  • A small story carries the words. Every fifth word, a line of gossip in Spanish with a translation gives a reason to keep going. All the vocabulary comes from that one story.

Research study · Lean Software Development · Innopolis University · 2023

Can small weekly goals make student software teams work better?

We tested an idea from Matthew Syed’s Black Box Thinking on six student teams over five weeks, and measured what changed.

My role
Co-author with two classmates. I worked across the whole study, made most of the design decisions and wrote much of the paper.
Method
Spike experiment with six student software teams, five weeks.
Status
Course paper, not submitted for publication.

The question

Aviation improves by studying its failures. One idea from the book is marginal gains: split a process into small parts, improve one part at a time and track whether the whole gets better. We asked whether that works for people building software together.

How we ran it

Week one was a baseline with no goal. In each later week, every team focused on one part of its process. Teams tracked member satisfaction, time in organisational meetings, test coverage and task counts. Satisfaction was our main measure, because it is the one that relates the practice directly to the people doing it.

  1. Week 1Baseline, no goalSatisfaction varied the most between teams.
  2. Week 2Organisational decisionsMeeting time went up first. The shortest meeting time rose by more than 50%.
  3. Week 3Communication and knowledge sharingSatisfaction kept rising and the spread between teams narrowed.
  4. Week 4Documentation and UI/UXReported test coverage dipped as teams widened what they counted, while writing fuller tests.
  5. Week 5Testing and code qualityHighest average satisfaction and the smallest spread. Test coverage rose significantly.

What we found

By the end, member satisfaction and test coverage had risen and meeting time had fallen. Teams that spent week two on organisation reported needing less meeting time afterwards, and team leads said they had more time to write code.

We left task counts out of the analysis. The number of tasks reflects how a team chooses to split work, not how hard the work was, so adding them across teams would have been misleading.

Limits, and what I would change

Six teams over five weeks, with one baseline week and no control group. We presented the result as insight, not proof. I would add a control group of teams with no weekly goal, run it for longer and include more teams, so the baseline week is not the only comparison.
